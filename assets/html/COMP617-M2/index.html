The $100,000 Question:
Who Really Pays?

A proposed fee hike on H-1B petitions would reshape American immigration — but its burden falls unevenly across industries, geographies, and employers large and small.

By Shengkai (Kevin) Tao  ·  Source: USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub  ·  h1b_datahubexport-2023.csv

For decades, the H-1B visa has been the front door to the American workforce for hundreds of thousands of skilled foreign workers each year. Engineers, doctors, professors, and nurses have passed through it — recruited by tech giants, research universities, rural hospitals, and small startups alike. Now, a proposed $100,000 fee per petition threatens to slam that door on the very employers least equipped to pay.

The politics of H-1B reform are familiar. But beneath the debate lies a less-examined reality: the program's users are not monolithic. The raw USCIS data tells a story of enormous variation — in who files, where they file, and what the stakes are for each of them.

Who Files the Most — and Who Can't Afford Not To

A handful of tech giants and IT outsourcing firms dominate H-1B filings by sheer volume. For these companies, a $100,000 fee would be a rounding error. The data makes clear that H-1B dependence is not a Silicon Valley story alone. It is a national story — written in the staffing decisions of hospitals in Montana, universities in Ohio, and engineering firms in Georgia. A $100,000 fee would be absorbed easily by the largest filers and absorbed painfully — or not at all — by the rest.

Policy, as always, is not neutral. The question is not whether to reform the H-1B system, but which vision of that system the reform is meant to serve.
